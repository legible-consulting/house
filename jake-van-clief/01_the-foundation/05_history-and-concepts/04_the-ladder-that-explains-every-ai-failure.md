# 2.4 The Ladder That Explains Every AI Failure

*The Foundation › History and Concepts · Author: Jake Van Clief · Source: YouTube (linked video, transcript on disk) plus written page · Length: 14:23 · Link: https://youtu.be/SjlCJIU9ODs*

## Summary

Jake argues that most people automate the wrong thing: they build custom tooling for a step that the next platform update will absorb for free. To decide where to invest instead, he offers a "ladder of abstraction" using books, movies and video games as three levels of the same story, each adding involvement and bespoke value. Applied to AI, the ladder runs from typing prompts, to building flows, to flows that generate data and improve. His advice is to give away what AI has made cheap, charge for the context-heavy layer above it, and keep asking which layer you stand on. The written page keeps the framework but reframes the business examples and predictions as unverified hypotheses.

## Core concepts

- **The trap is wrong-layer automation:** the risk is not being replaced by AI but spending months on a capability the next model or platform release will provide natively.
- **Value lives in process, not output:** a website, a document or a script is the "book" version; the lasting value is in how steps connect, which need a human, which a tool handles better and which to leave alone.
- **Each layer reprices the one below:** a new abstraction layer does not kill the layer beneath, it changes what that layer is worth and becomes part of the infrastructure.
- **Context is the moat:** domain knowledge, client relationships, proprietary data and judgment cannot be scraped into a general model because they exist only in your situation.
- **Give away the commodity, sell the involved layer:** cheap output is the door opener; pay is for the part that needs taste, research and trust.
- **Break work apart first:** his consulting starts by splitting each department's tasks into AI-suitable, traditional-tool-suitable and human-only.
- **Value migrates, markets endure:** he argues automation kills specific middleman positions rather than markets, and that new layers have always created new work.

## Frameworks and methods

1. Books, movies, video games: a static output, a produced experience, a responsive participatory one; used as an analogy only, not a ranking of financial returns (page).
2. Three layers of AI use: layer one is prompting a chat, layer two is building flows tied to your own processes, layer three is flows that produce evidence you review and use to improve instructions. The page adds that data collection alone does not make a system improve itself; a person reviews and tests changes.
3. Task triage per department: where AI helps, where traditional tooling is better, where a person is needed.
4. Three self-check questions (page): which layer am I on, will this become a feature of someone else's tool soon, what do I know or have access to that exists only in my context.
