# LESSON 3.2: The Questions Schools Should Be Asking

*The Archive › Module 3: Ownership In the AI Age · Author: Jake Van Clief · Source: text lesson (essay with course guide)*

## Summary

Jake argues that schools and universities keep the intellectual property their teachers and students produce, and that AI sharpens the problem. He proposes that each student train a personal AI over their degree and graduate owning it and its data, that teachers own and license their methods, and that institutions take a share rather than the whole asset. The essay is a policy argument, anchored in his mother's decade of unowned lesson plans. The course guide turns it into a practical inventory exercise and warns that ownership rules vary by jurisdiction and employment terms.

## Core concepts

- **Value flows upward:** Jake says institutions capture the value of expertise, so "AI makes you more valuable" stays potential unless individuals own what they build.
- **Portable personal AI:** trained on years of one person's work, it becomes an asset that travels with them, but only if they own it and the data privately.
- **Licensing instead of employment:** a company could license someone's trained AI instead of hiring one person for limited hours.
- **Reframed questions:** replace "how do we use AI in class" with who owns the trained AI, how teachers benefit from innovations, and how revenue from institutional knowledge is shared.
- **Precedents:** code hosting, music distribution and newsletter platforms are cited as examples of creators owning the asset.

## Frameworks and methods

The essay's argument:

1. Teachers and professors create valuable IP the institution owns.
2. Graduates in technical fields already show portfolios; AI makes the portfolio an active asset.
3. A student-trained personal AI could be that asset, if ownership and privacy are guaranteed.
4. That makes individuals into small licensors, with schools taking a minority share.
5. Three structural shifts follow: portable AI ownership, teacher IP rights, revenue sharing.
6. The institution that offers this first gains a strong recruiting pitch.

The guide's practical version: inventory the notes, instructions, outputs and model service in a fictional project, noting for each item its supplier, who has access and how it can be exported; leave unclear rights marked unknown, and test whether a different tool could use the exports.

## Claims and evidence

- Teachers' lesson plans are fully institution-owned — personal example from Florida — varies by jurisdiction; the guide cites US work-made-for-hire rules (Copyright Circular 30) depend on circumstances.
- A student-trained AI could be licensed — speculative proposal, no evidence of a working model.
- Portable files do not transfer ownership of a provider's model — the guide's correction.
