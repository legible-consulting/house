# Brooke Hays - Understanding Cognitive Functions

*David's Corner › Brooke Hays: The Lens Series · Author: Brooke Hays · Source: text lesson*

## Summary

Brooke Hays introduces cognitive functions as a way to describe how people take in information and how they decide what to do with it, rather than a way to sort people into types. She sets out the eight functions, how they pair up, and how a person's four are ranked into a stack. Her advice is to work out your own pattern by observing reasoning over weeks, and to distrust quick tests and four-letter codes. The framing throughout is lens versus label: a tool for curiosity, not for certainty. The page is the groundwork for her follow-on lesson on applying the same ideas to AI agents.

## Core concepts

- **Lens, not label:** the framework highlights some patterns and hides others. Experience, culture, maturity, neurodiversity and circumstances also shape people, so no one fits a type perfectly.
- **Perceiving and judging:** every person does both. Perceiving takes in information (concrete facts, or abstract patterns and possibilities); judging decides what to do with it (logic and process, or values and human impact).
- **External and internal attitudes:** each function faces outward or inward. She uses these words instead of the older extraversion and introversion terms, because the technical meaning is about focus of attention, not sociability.
- **The eight functions:** four perceiving and four judging, each with an external and internal form. She illustrates each with small thought-process examples (an apple, a crying friend) credited to a source she lists as further reading.
- **Pairs and the stack:** functions come in fixed pairs, so a person has one of two perceiving pairs and one of two judging pairs. Four functions are ranked from most natural to least, giving sixteen possible stacks.
- **Observe reasoning, not decisions:** two people can make the same choice for different cognitive reasons, so the reasoning is the data.

## Frameworks and methods

1. Split mental activity into perceiving and judging.
2. Divide perceiving into concrete versus abstract information and judging into logic versus values.
3. Give each an outward and an inward form, making eight functions.
4. Pair them (each perceiving function with its opposite form of the other kind, and likewise for judging), so each person holds four.
5. Rank the four into a stack: hero, parent, child, inferior, where the hero is the most natural, the parent supports it from the opposite side (perceiving versus judging, outward versus inward), the child balances the parent, and the inferior is the typical weak point. The page notes other texts use different names.
6. Convert the stack into a short letter code, noting that the code is lossy and the function stack is more reliable than the letters.
7. To type yourself, answer observation questions: what do you notice first, how do you decide, what do you ask instinctively, what mistakes repeat, how do you approach unfamiliar problems, and what feels effortless or draining.

## Claims and evidence

- Most personality tests capture self-presentation rather than thinking — author's view, no evidence given.
- Mistyping is common and typing yourself can take weeks or months — author's experience.
- Four letters can mislead more than a function stack — author's view; the framework itself is a contested theory in personality psychology, so treat claims about it as descriptive models, not findings.
- The page's own notes concede that views differ on whether people can use functions outside their stack.
