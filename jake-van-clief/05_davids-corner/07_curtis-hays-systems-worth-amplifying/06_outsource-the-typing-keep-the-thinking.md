# Outsource the Typing, Keep the Thinking

*David's Corner › Curtis Hays - Systems Worth Amplifying · Author: Curtis Hays · Source: text lesson*

## Summary

Curtis argues that AI advantage is not a tools problem, because everyone has the same models and public data. What nobody else has is the thinking that already happened in your own conversations and in the thinkers you have absorbed. He builds on his co-host's line that writing is the thinking and the keyboard work is only typing, so you should delegate typing and keep thinking. The practical advice is to capture conversations, ask AI for patterns across many of them rather than summaries, and start with a single unsorted notebook. A caution on the page notes that pattern-reading is a clue to examine with the person, not proof of motive.

## Core concepts

- **Public intelligence is the floor:** anything every operator can pull in seconds (deep research, competitive analysis) cannot differentiate you.
- **Writing is thinking, typing is not:** the asset is the hours or years of thought behind a piece; the tool should do only the final transcription.
- **Asymmetric data:** two kinds nobody else can scrape: private conversations (client calls, debriefs, voice memos) and your personal synthesis of the thinkers you have taken in, including which ideas you rejected.
- **Vacuum fills with the average:** with no doctrine of your own, a system defaults to training data and borrowed templates, so the thinking still happens but is done by defaults.
- **Pattern over summary:** a summary replays what you know; asking what a run of decisions reveals about what someone believes surfaces things no single transcript shows.
- **Surface, don't name:** the machine should lay out related entries but leave naming the belief to the human; empty is better than fabricated.

## Frameworks and methods

The argument, in order:

1. Tool and data parity means AI advantage is not in the stack.
2. The real asset is thinking, and thinking leaves residue in conversations.
3. Most operators let that residue evaporate by carrying insight in their heads.
4. Capture conversations and track the thinkers who shaped you.
5. Query the corpus for patterns of decision, not for recaps; the example client was found to decide on downside protection, not upside, only after reading a dozen conversations together.
6. When a pattern contradicts what someone says about themselves, trust the pattern.
7. Start low-tech: one notebook, entries go in the next blank space, no to-do lists, review constantly so old entries collide.
8. Add structure only if it helps retrieval and collision rather than burying notes.

## Claims and evidence

- Public data and tools cannot be a moat — reasoning from parity, no data.
- A home care operator's decisions favoured blame avoidance over upside — a single client anecdote, details anonymised; the Reading Guide notes patterns are clues to examine with the person.
- Commonplace practices were used by well-known figures — historical references, not checked.
- Authors' own transcripts yielded his worldview more clearly than he could have written it cold — self-report.
