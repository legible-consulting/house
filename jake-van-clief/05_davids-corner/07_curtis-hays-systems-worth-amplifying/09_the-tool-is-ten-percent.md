# The Tool Is Ten Percent

*David's Corner › Curtis Hays - Systems Worth Amplifying · Author: Curtis Hays · Source: text lesson*

## Summary

Curtis opens with a COO who imagined abolishing a process entirely, which ended a build-versus-buy debate because nobody could say what would break. He uses that to argue that tools fail when the purpose and process beneath them were never written down. He adapts Jake Van Clief's 60/30/10 split into non-technical terms (truth, process, tool), supports it with two studies on why AI initiatives fail, and ends with four questions to ask before any purchase. The learner can leave with a short pre-purchase checklist, and a caution about leaning on contested percentages.

## Core concepts

- **The stop question:** if a process vanished tomorrow, what specific thing breaks? A room that cannot answer does not yet know the process's purpose, and software would only make it faster.
- **Sixty, thirty, ten:** Jake's technical ratio (database queries, rules, AI) is reframed for operators as written-down truth, explicit process, and the interchangeable tool. In Jake's phrasing, "The 10% makes the whole thing feel intelligent. The 90% makes it actually work."
- **Truth depends on the system:** for market-facing work it is customer truth; for internal processes it is truth about the business and its people.
- **Only the tool can be bought:** the tool has a demo and a price, whereas truth and process have to be excavated from inside the business by people who know it.
- **Written layers survive vendor change:** truth and process documented once carry over to the next tool.
- **Statistics are contested:** the widely quoted failure figure measures zero measurable return, not failure, and its authors flag a short observation window. Curtis prefers the causes over the percentages.

## Frameworks and methods

The argument, in order:

1. Software purchases go unused when nobody can say what the work is for.
2. The stop question tests whether purpose is known.
3. Map the split: truth (what is real and why the process exists), process (who does what, handling exceptions, so two people make the same call), tool (the swappable part).
4. Check external research: one study reports few pilots reaching production; another interviews practitioners and blames problem framing, data and chasing novelty over user need.
5. Show both studies point to truth and process, not model quality, as the failure source.
6. Apply four pre-purchase questions: what breaks if this stopped; would two people decide the same way; does the job need meaning or could a formula do it; if the pilot succeeds, which named capability appears next year.
7. Conclusion: write down truth and process before buying the tool.

## Tools, settings and resources

- **MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide (July 2025)** — cited report; the page links a PDF; preliminary, with a stated six-month window limitation.
- **RAND study of AI project failure (2024)** — interviews with experienced practitioners; five root causes named; the page links a report.
- **Jake's 60/30/10 lesson** — the source of the ratio, linked in the Skool classroom.

## Claims and evidence

- 95 percent of AI efforts fail — the page corrects this to zero measurable return among organisations, drawn from a preliminary report; a stricter test than failure. Do not cite as a failure rate.
- Six in ten investigated, two in ten piloted and one in twenty reached production — from the same report, among organisations weighing custom or vendor tools; useful as a survival curve, still preliminary.
- Leadership and problem framing cause more AI failures than technical limits — RAND study, 65 interviews; sample is practitioners, not a measured failure population.
- The COO could not answer the stop question — single client anecdote; reading guide notes the ratio is a planning lens, not a universal allocation.

## Apply to icm-workshop

- **S4 quote and S1 prospecting:** adopt the stop question as a qualifying question when a prospect asks for a tool. Put it in the client-research contract (S2) as a first-call prompt: what would break if this process disappeared? That helps Jamie enter the call knowing the trade.
- **O1 tool research verdicts:** adopt the four questions as the checklist behind each adopt, watch or decline verdict, and record the answers with a recheck date. This keeps the tool as the replaceable ten percent and the written layers in the folder.
- **C1 shared core and boundaries:** truth and process written once, tools swappable, is the "one home per fact" boundary applied to vendors. Adopt as the stated reason tool choices are in a separate stack file rather than woven through rules.
- **D4 conformance:** the thirty-percent question (two people, same call) is a good test for whether a stage contract is explicit enough to start cold; adapt for contract review.
