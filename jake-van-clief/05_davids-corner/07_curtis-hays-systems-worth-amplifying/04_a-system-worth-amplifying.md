# A System Worth Amplifying

*David's Corner › Curtis Hays - Systems Worth Amplifying · Author: Curtis Hays · Source: text lesson*

## Summary

Curtis argues that building an agent architecture is not enough: a folder system is an instrument, and what makes its output distinctive is the written set of beliefs and rules (the doctrine) that agents are held to. Using a piano story and a client case, he distinguishes SOPs, which teach agents how to do the work, from doctrine, which says what the work is for and which work to refuse. He describes how he found his own doctrine by mining years of podcast transcripts, and he closes with a challenge: if someone cloned your stack tomorrow, what would still be yours? The learner leaves with the idea of writing down earned rules before adding more agents.

## Core concepts

- **Instrument versus score:** the folder system, router, specialists and memory are the instrument; doctrine is what is played on it. Without it the system produces competent but generic output.
- **SOPs versus doctrine:** an SOP-loaded agent writes a flawless follow-up; a doctrine-loaded agent notices the follow-up should not be sent at all. Skill versus being right.
- **Doctrine is earned by repetition:** rules he had applied for years as judgment were doctrine without a name. Writing them down is a recognition exercise more than a creative one.
- **AI as recognition amplifier:** feeding his own transcripts to a model surfaced patterns he had said repeatedly but never named.
- **Brief is rarely the problem:** the stated request sits downstream of an issue the client often cannot articulate; asking why the customer buys is the upstream move.
- **Controlled doctrine layers:** the doctrine is treated as the asset, delivered through guarded surfaces rather than given away.

## Frameworks and methods

The argument, in order:

1. A polished system with named agents can still produce generic work, and the cause is not architecture.
2. The architecture is the instrument; the operating beliefs are the score.
3. SOPs shape execution; doctrine shapes whether and what to execute. Two agents with the same SOPs behave differently when only one holds doctrine.
4. Most experienced operators already hold doctrine in their heads, formed by repeated decisions, but have never written it.
5. Three rules summarise the order of work: coherence before creativity, diagnosis before tactics, context before architecture.
6. Case: a services owner sold by listening and diagnosing, while his team handed prospects a long proposal, so the brand promise broke at the handoff. The fix is to codify the owner's sales approach as doctrine, not another SOP.
7. In his own build, a router sends work, a doctrine layer is loaded before producing, a verifier checks output that the producer cannot close itself, and memory stays as human-readable files.
8. Therefore build the score first, then the system that protects it.

## Tools, settings and resources

- **Orchestrator plus specialist agents in folders** — his architecture, with a doctrine layer loaded before work.
- **MCP server** — his channel for delivering doctrine into clients' AI environments — described as separate from the internal doctrine layer.
- **Podcast transcripts mined with AI** — method for extracting patterns — author's own process.

## Claims and evidence

- Doctrine, not architecture, drives distinctive output — argued through a piano anecdote and one unnamed client; no measurement.
- Deals were failing at the sales handoff — client case, diagnosed by the author, outcome of the doctrine fix not yet known.
- Memory kept in folders rather than a vector database keeps doctrine legible a year later — design preference, no comparison offered.
- Producers are the worst auditors of their own output — stated principle, no evidence.
